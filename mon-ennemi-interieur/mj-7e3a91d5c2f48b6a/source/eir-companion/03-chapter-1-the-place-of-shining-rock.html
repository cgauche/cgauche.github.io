Chapter 1 the Place of Shining Rock

On the road to Middenheim, about 50 miles from the city, there is a remote shrine known as the Place of Shining Rock. Unusually, it is sacred to both Ulric and Sigmar, and the two cults coexist here peacefully, as they have done for thousands of years.

The Place of Shining Rock features in Empire in Ruins (chapter 3), as the Characters attend a meeting between delegations from Altdorf and Middenheim. This chapter presents more detailed information on the shrine, along with some ideas that can be used to add complication to the events, or give Characters reason to return to the shrine at a later time.

These events serve to underscore the differences of opinion between the most stringent and outspoken Sigmarites and Ulricans, and can serve to underline this conflict. Conversely, the shrine is also a place of union between the two Gods. Spending time here may help to remind Characters who are strongly partisan towards one god or the other that compromise between the two faiths is an ideal worth striving towards.

Reaching the Shrine

The Characters will visit the shrine during the events of Empire in Ruins, as delegations from Altdorf and Middenheim meet in an attempt to calm rising tensions between Ulrican and Sigmarite factions. However, during Power Behind the Throne they will also pass the site on their way to Middenheim, and it may help to add to the sense of gravitas the site enjoys if the Characters are already familiar with it before Empire in Ruins.

Any time that the Characters are travelling between Middenheim and Altdorf, they will pass by the road that leads to the Place of Shining Rock. Unless they have a reason to go there, though, they will probably overlook the place — they do have an Empire to save, after all. Here are a few possible ways to weave a visit to the shrine into the plot of the adventure, especially if you would like the Characters to visit it before Empire in Ruins .

Ust Passing by J

The Characters will have to travel between Altdorf and Middenheim at least once in the course of the campaign, which will take them past the side-road that leads to the shrine. If the party includes devout followers of Sigmar and/or Ulric, they may decide to visit, pay their respects, and pray — especially if one or more Characters has received Sin Points from either deity.

A Research Mission

By now, the Characters will no doubt be aware of the broad outlines of the doctrinal differences that have led to the growing conflict between the cults of Sigmar and Ulric. They may have learned part of the story from the scholar-monk Father Marcus in ‘Vengeance of the Gravelord,’ or they may have picked up bits and pieces along the way. ( Death on the Reik Companion , pages 114–20.)

They will probably have heard, as well, that Sigmarite and Ulrican scholars are combing through ancient records and later works of theology in the hope of finding an underlying truth upon which both sides can agree, and which can provide a basis for peace. As a shrine sacred to both gods, the Place of Shining Rock will be of great interest to these theologians.

In the course of their adventures, the Characters have become acquainted with one or more distinguished theologians. Sigmarites might be monks from Father Marcus’s monastery near Dunkelberg, or from the other renowned monastery at Priestlichheim, or scholars from the Department of Theology at the University of Altdorf ( Altdorf , page 159). Their Ulrican counterparts will almost certainly be from Middenheim’s Collegium Theologica ( Middenheim , page 44).

Whatever their faith and wherever they come from, these scholars will need an escort on the dangerous highway through the Drak Wald, and although they expect that their research in the shrine’s archives will go smoothly and quickly, who is to say what they will find once they get there?

The Relic

The Characters are approached by priests of both religions, and asked to bring one chest of Ulrican relics and one chest of Sigmarite relics to the Place of Shining Rock, along with a sufficient cash donation to permit the expansion of the existing buildings and the construction of a new reliquary chapel. The priests’ aim is to promote peace between the two faiths by raising the profile of their shared holy place, highlighting its spiritual importance, and turning this sacred site into a popular destination for pilgrims.

For Grognards: a Stolen Shard

In ‘Vengeance of the Gravelord’ in the Death on the Reik Companion (see pages 114–20), the Characters encountered Father Marcus, a scholar-monk whom Harald Gräber, the necromancer, tried to assassinate. In the original ‘Carrion Up the Reik’, Father Marcus revealed to the Characters that he had spent some of his time as a novice serving at the shrine, and when he left he took a tiny shard of the stone with him . As time went on he felt more and more guilty about it, and resolved to make a pilgrimage to return the shard to its rightful place. However, he was prevented from doing so by pressing concerns elsewhere, and asked the Characters to return the shard for him.

If you are reading this before you have run Power Behind the Throne , Father Marcus could give the Characters this little quest while they are travelling together on the river. The Characters might stop off on their way to Middenheim during P ower Behind the Throne , although they will probably not want to delay their journey. They might tell themselves that they will keep their promise to the kindly old monk on their way back to Altdorf — which, of course, would be in Empire in Ruins .

Of course, there is always the chance that Father Marcus survived the Gravelord’s attack on the Characters’ boat, and their paths crossed at some other point in the campaign. So long as the Characters’ immediate travel plans will take them along the Altdorf to Middenheim road, he can still ask them for the same favour. Even if Father Marcus is dead, the Characters might run into his two subordinates, Brother Martin or Brother Rolf (page 12), who tell them of the good Father’s dying wish that the shard be returned to its rightful place.

Relics and Miracle

When using holy relics in a game, as the GM, you should take some time to design them. Every relic needs a form, a backstory, and specific effects, while the most powerful can require almost as much detail as an NPC, reflecting the aspects of their deity’s personality and interests that are channelled through them into the world. Like enchanted items and other supernatural objects, powerful relics are extremely rare in the Old World — but of course, there is no shortage of charlatans and other enterprising types who will be only too happy to sell a lock of Sigmar’s hair, a vial of Shallya’s tears, or a tooth lost by Ulric while fighting Daemons in the form of a wolf.

When a relic is genuine, it should have some supernatural effects that are in keeping with its patron’s personality and with a level of power appropriate for its size, form, and degree of sanctity. As GM, you have complete freedom to design relics according to the needs of your campaign, but here are a few ideas to help get you started.

The Power of Prayer

A relic can empower its wielder’s prayers, adding 1–3 SLs to the result of any Pray Test to the deity in question. Rolls on the Wrath of the Gods Table ( WFRP , page 218) have a modifier between –10 and –30: a result of zero or less means that the Wrath of the Gods has been avoided.

Signs of Favour

When a Pray Test for a Miracle is successful, the Character gains a Blessing of the patron deity (GM’s choice) in addition to the Miracle they sought. If the Test is a Critical success, two Blessings are gained.

Relics of Note

Of course, there are those who want to prevent this from happening. Some fanatics, when they hear of the plan, will stop at nothing to seize the relics, intending to take those of their own god to a suitable, unshared temple. The relics of the rival deity are regarded as heretical and must be destroyed. Bandits will be happy to relieve the Characters of the cash donation, and will probably find some sale value in the relics as well. They may pretend to be followers of one deity or the other in order to disarm the Characters’ suspicions and get close enough to attack.

The relics themselves are secured in stout chests and protected by multiple locks. They may be genuine, and may even have some religious power (see Relics of Note ). More likely, they will be fakes: harmless bones and other objects that have become associated with a particular legend from the lore of one of the gods, but which are, in fact, completely mundane and unrelated.

Some relics are deliberate fakes, while others may have been given to a temple in the honest belief that they are genuine. Worst of all, the relics may actually be artefacts of Chaos, and the priests who approached the Characters may be cultists in disguise. Upon reaching the shrine and opening the chests, the Characters may find themselves faced with Warpstone that bathes them all in deadly radiation, or Daemons cast into the forms of ancient objects — a form that will be dispelled by the holy ground of the shrine.

The Skull of Englebrecht the Wise

Englebrecht the Wise was a scholar of Sigmar who died in 1450 IC. A devout worshipper and acomplished scholar, Englebrecht spend much of his life in a small monastry near Desidorf, compiling notes on the various histories of Sigmar, writing theological missives, and caring for a small collection of chickens. Englebrecht eventually died when a candle he was writing by tipped over, burning down much of the monastry. The only surviving artefacts from Englebrechts room were his skull, miraculously unmarred by the flames, and his final writings. Though a minor figure in the church, Englebrecht's skull is nevertheless a holy object. Anyone possessing the skull is immune to the Ablaze condition.

The Axehead of Margritte

A warrior priest of Ulric, Margritte died in Brettonia during one of the many campaigns the Empire has fought there. The axehead is all that remains of her weapon, shattered upon the enchanted armour of a grail knight. Though now ruined, the fragmets of the axehead carry a potent mote of Margritte's unwavering faith in Ulric. Anyone bearing even a piece of Margritte's axe adds +1 to the damage of any axe they weild.

Shard of the Shining Rock

A tiny fragment blasphemously removed from the relic of Shining Rock, this piece of black obsidian has now power of its own. However, should it be returned to the shrine by a devotee of either Sigmar or Ulric, that person may remove any Sin Points they have currently accumulated. If they have no Sin Points, they may instead add the Blessed Talent to their current career.

The Shrine

As the Altdorf to Middenheim road winds through the forest between Kutenholz and the Forest Hearth Inn, there is a clear side-turning marked by a large, white boulder. The wolf’s-head symbol of Ulric is carved on one side of the boulder; Sigmar’s hammer is carved on the other. Both carvings are defaced as though someone has swung at them several times with a hammer or mace.

Any Dwarf, or anyone who makes a successful Trade (Stonemason) Test, can tell that the damage is recent and no more than a week old. It looks like a regrettable sign of the times.

The Legend of Shining Rock

The shrine is built on the site where the god (either Ulric or Sigmar, depending on the faith of the source) had paused to rest after a battle against the forces of Chaos but was ambushed.

In both versions of the legend, the god was weaponless. In the Sigmarite myth, the battle took place before Sigmar received Ghal Maraz from Kurgan Ironbeard, and his sword had shattered against the armour of the last Champion of Chaos he killed. In the Ulrican version, the god had been fighting in wolf form, chasing down the minions of Chaos who had stolen his great axe Blitzbeil.

In both tales, the god picked up a strange, glittering rock to shield himself from his attackers. One of their blows shattered part of it into a long sharp shard and, using it as a sword, he killed five hundred of his enemies before they fled.

The legend is well-known, and any Character who makes an Easy (+40) Lore (Theology) Test will recall its broad outlines. Sigmarite Characters will have heard a version where their patron deity is the hero, and Ulricans will know a version that casts the wolf-god in the same role.

Everyone knows that two versions of the legend exist. Ulricans claim that their version is older, and that the tale was co-opted by Sigmarite Reiklanders after the first Emperor was deified. Sigmarite doctrine maintains that their version is the authentic one, but a few will concede that he might have had Ulric’s help in the battle — in the form of supernatural aid, though, and not in person.

Half a mile down the turning is an open area of flattish rocky ground, just over an acre in extent. A simple yet elegant building stands in the middle of the clearing. It is larger than a normal wayside shrine, and not unlike the temples typically found in larger villages: about 40-ft long with small and clearly symbolic crenellations and a low tower at one end. A narrow river runs along one side of the clearing, and the dense forest continues beyond it. The priests’ house stands at the edge of the clearing next to the river, about 70 yd from the shrine itself. This well kept building is smaller, lower and more functional, with shuttered windows.

Inside the Shrine

The interior of the shrine is dominated by a long, thin outcrop of pale rock, which glitters and glistens in the light of two dozen large lamps that hang from the ceiling. The lamps’ brass bodies are marked with the alternating signs of the wolf ’s head and the twin-tailed comet, and the imagery of Sigmar and Ulric alternates in a similar fashion throughout.

There are no pews or prayer-stools in the shrine: the floor is clean but completely bare. Any of the attending priests can explain that this is because the Sigmarite way is to stand or kneel on bare stone, so they did not require pews; the Ulricans, not wanting to appear softer or less devoted, also eschewed their customary use of pews.

At the far end of the shrine, the ceiling rises in an octagonal tower. Beneath the centre of the tower is the highest part of the rock outcrop, which stands about3 ft above floor level. A sharp break indicates where, according to the legends of both faiths, the god snapped a piece off the outcrop to defend himself.

An impressive statue of Ulric, depicted holding a shard of rock, stands against the back wall . As in most of Ulric’s temples, an eternal flame burns in a circular stone fire-pit in front of the statue. On entering the shrine, the sound of the flame flickering is audible, whilst the smell of the fire lingers and the temperature drops significantly.

An altar to Sigmar stands in the south-eastern part of this area, positioned so that worshippers facing it are looking in the direction of Karaz-a-Karak, the Dwarven citadel where, it is believed, Sigmar went on his last journey as a mortal. This alignment is common in Sigmarite temples, especially older ones.

Interior walls separate the public area of the shrine from two robing rooms: one for Sigmarite priests; the other for Ulrican priests. Beneath each is a small underground treasury, reached by a steep stone staircase under a trapdoor, where offerings and valuable ritual equipment are kept.

THE PL ACE OF SHINING ROCK

The Place of Shining Rock

The Priests’ Quarters

This low, plain building accommodates the shrine’s complement of a dozen priests: six from each faith. Unusually, the Place of Shining Rock does not have any guest accommodation. Visitors are encouraged to pray in the shrine, leave a donation, and continue their journey. The priests have no objection to visitors pitching tents in the clearing for a night or two, but anyone planning on staying longer will arouse their suspicions. The shrine is a simple one, and there is nothing much to do here once one has prayed and donated.

The Hall

Double doors open onto a common hall and refectory. It is plainly furnished with two long, wooden tables flanked by benches.

The room is so plain that it is austere and looks unfinished. Apart from a simple carved frieze around the top of the wall, decorated with alternating symbols of Ulric and Sigmar, everything is undecorated plaster and wood. A successful Charm Test may convince a Sigmarite priest to confess — privately, and not within the hearing of any other priest — that the lavish decoration found in other buildings associated with Sigmar’s worship has been neglected because the Sigmarites do not want to give the Ulricans any cause to echo the common northern sentiment that Reiklanders are all soft southern fops.

The Dormitories

To either side of the main hall is a door marked, respectively, with the hammer of Sigmar and the wolf ’s-head of Ulric. Each door leads to a dormitory accommodating six priests. These rooms are slightly more relaxed in their décor: the Ulrican dormitory is plain but comfortable; the Sigmarite counterpart is decorated tastefully, but not too lavishly.

The Kitchen and Stores

At the back of the main hall is a simple kitchen and store-room where meals are prepared and foodstuffs are stored.

The Priests

As mentioned already, the Place of Shining Rock is attended by priests of two faiths, making it almost unique in the Empire. Father Gregor Braun leads the priests of Sigmar. High priest Wolf-born Fernand Wagner commands those of Ulric. The priests are divided into three shifts, each consisting of two priests from each faith: while one shift prays in the chapel, another sleeps, and the third keeps a lookout for trouble from the forest — detaching two members to cook and serve meals as necessary. The shifts change every eight hours — this way the shrine is attended day and night.

The Place of Shining Rock is seen as a hard posting, with few luxuries. Only the most devout from either faith volunteer to serve there, either to test their devotion or to uphold the One True Faith in the face of the other cult. Despite the rivalry, there is seldom any conflict between the two contingents. The shrine is so unique in the Empire’s religious fabric that any kind of bad behaviour would embarrass one cult in front of its archrival, and bring down the wrath of the cult’s leaders upon the offender.

Priest Silvie Unterfaust

Silvie Unterfaust has only recently arrived at Shining Rock, but in her short time there has gained a reputation for arrogance among the Ulrican contingent. Used to the pomp and ceremony of the Grand Cathedral in Altdorf, she fails to see why so much time and space is devoted to Ulric. Doubtless Unterfaut was a poor choice for assignment to Shining Rock, but her superiors in Altdorf had grown tired of her increasingly grating tirades on the importance of devotion and found reason to send her away from the city.

Father Gregor Braun

The young priest insists of promoting Sigmar over Ulric at every opportunity, often in obnoxious ways, but her devotion is genuine. Most look to Father Braun to bring her in line, but so far he has failed to do so.

The chief Sigmarite priest of the temple of Shining Rock, Gregor Braun seeks to represent the very best characteristics of a devout worshiper of Sigmar. His armour is always perfectly maintained, his hammer a properly weighted weapon and in no way ceremonial, and his demeanor always stoic and aloof. Behind this façade lies a man of simple faith and good common sense, who can nevertheless sometimes be goaded into unwise actions when he feels the cult’s honour is being questioned.

Priest Manfred Ulricson

Ulricson became a priest not out of devotion, but because it was a reasonable career opportunity for the third son of a middling farmer. That is not to say he is not a faithful man — he sharpens his axe like any Ulrican — but he does perhaps lack the passion shared by some of his fellows. Ulricson remains a practical man, however, and sees carefully to his duties. He knows that Shining Rock is an important position, and he hopes to take Wolf-Born Wagner’s place as head priest when the time comes.

Wolf-Born Fernand Wagner

Fernand Wagner came to Middenheim as a child, borne there by merchants from the south. Though his parents kept to their own faith, Fernand found a welcome in the church that he did not always find in his fellow children. He eventually joined the cult of Ulric as a novitiate, and rose to prominence both because of his devotion and due to his keen mind. He earned the right to wear a wolf-pelt at the age of 15, and though he drew the attention of several knightly orders, his heart was set on becoming a priest.

Wagner is devoted to Ulric, but recognises the respect due to the other gods. He enjoys seeing to the spiritual needs of the pilgrims who come to Shining Rock, and though he is increasingly distracted by matters of cult politics he does not neglect these duties. Unfortunately, Wagner has come to expect this same respect from all who tend to Shining Rock, and has no time for those who fail to properly respect the dual traditions of the shrine. He has noticed that certain Sigmarite priests are attempting to upset this delicate balance. Though not driven to act just yet, it has occurred to the Wolf-Born that what the hammer fails to temper, the wolf might rightly rend asunder.

Still, he does find the life of a priest somewhat stifling, and is quick to indulge in bouts of gambling and drunkenness when he thinks he can get away with it. So far this has not drawn the ire of his superiors, but Ulricson knows that this will only remain the case so long as things run smoothly at the Shrine. He has spent long hours attempting to convince the Sigmarite priest Unterfaust that there are easier ways to go about promotion ones faith, but so far the woman has ignored him. Ulricson is at his wits end, and would prefer for things to quite down so he can go back to playing cards with the less devout sort of pilgrim.

Return to Shining Rock

Having first met them on a barge headed towards Altdorf, it is likely that the head of their monastry, Father Marcus, has perished. Indeed, one of the Brothers may even have joined him. The survivor, or hopefully survivors, are intent on preserving the reputation of the later head of their monastry, and so were shocked to discover that he had in his possessions a piece of relic at Shining Rock.

Discrete inqueries have demonstrated that Father Marcus had no right to the relic, and the implication that he may have damaged it in the course of stealing a shard is deeply troubling. They have resovled to keep this piece of information to themselves, and seek to return the shard to Shining Rock. If he is still alive, Father Marcus will have come to the conclusion that the shard must be returned as well.

Unfortunately, neighter have good reason to travel to Shining Rock — certainly not in an official capacity. They are desperete to see the Shard returned, and will engage the Characters to help them in doing so. Thoguh neither is wealthy — and stealing as cult funds would only make spiritual matters even worse — they can offer only a single gold crown as payment. However, they assure the Characters that the spiritual rewards, in stark contrast to the meagre worldly ones, will be immesureable.

Adventure Ideas

In a time of conflict between the Empire’s two most powerful religions, the unique shrine at the Place of Shining Rock takes on an even greater significance. A wide range of people can be encountered here, and the highly-charged religious and political significance of the place means that almost any encounter can turn dangerous very quickly.

The One True Faith

In more peaceful times, the Place of Shining Rock is a symbol of peace and coexistence between the faiths of Sigmar and Ulric, with religious differences amounting to no more than good-natured banter. With the Empire on the brink of civil war, though, things are different.

A mob of fanatics invades the shrine, intent on slaughtering the priests of the rival faith and destroying all the statues, altars, and other signs of that faith’s existence. The mob might be Ulricans led by Sons of Ulric or even by Wolf-kin (see the Middenheim , page 154) or Sigmarites led by flagellants, warrior priests, or even witch hunters. Anyone who resists, regardless of the faith they profess, is decried by the mob’s leaders as a heretic or an apostate, and runs a significant risk of being torn to pieces. Anyone who fails to join in with loud and enthusiastic cries of devotion runs a risk themselves — only slightly less — of being punished for their lack of zeal by being beaten unconscious and having their possessions seized by the mob’s leaders.

Empire in Ruins to represent Use the profile found on page 18 of these fantastics. The mob’s leaders will be in the upper two levels of their careers, and will have Talents reflecting their fanaticism and ability to stir up a mob: Commanding Presence , Hatred (the other faith), Holy Hatred , Impassioned Zeal , Inspiring , and Public Speaker are all useful.

The Characters can respond to the appearance of a mob in various ways, depending on their skills and preferences. The situation could turn into a defence of the shrine and its clearing, with the Characters leading a motley force of priests, monks, and visiting pilgrims against the howling mob.

More cerebral parties might try to defuse tensions before things turn violent, making opposed Charm Tests with the mob’s leaders in the hope of calming things down —or just playing for time until help arrives in the form of a noble visitor and his escort whom the priests are expecting. This visitor might even be the Grand Theogonist himself (see box), and his escort of templars will be sufficient to even the odds. Players who take this option should be prepared to roleplay their speeches and arguments, and you should apply modifiers to their Tests based on how eloquently they have spoken.

If everything goes horribly wrong and both the shrine and the Characters appear to be doomed, there is always the possibility of divine intervention. Everyone in the mob will be accumulating Sin Points with every action, and if their leaders try making Pray Tests they may well incur the Wrath of the Gods ( WFRP , pages 218–19). More spectacular interventions, even at a shrine like this one, are not common in the Old World, and should be used with great restraint. If possible, they should look like accidents or chance happenings, but the Characters to whom they happen — especially if they are devout — should be left wondering whether the hand of a deity was involved.

To make a volatile situation even worse, a mob of fanatics from the opposing religion might arrive at the same time, or shortly thereafter.

Let There Be Peace

More peaceful, but equally inconvenient, might be a wandering mob of peace-seeking pilgrims who have decided to stay at the Place of Shining Rock, praying continuously to both gods until they intervene and resolve the crisis. While their intentions are good, they quickly turn the clearing around the shrine into a muddy quagmire with insufficient sanitation and rampant disease. The notes on the camp in Chapter 3, ‘Journey to Shining Rock’ will be useful in this scenario.

These wandering idealists take over the shrine, politely but firmly refusing entry to the attending priests unless they adopt the agenda of round-the-clock prayer and song themselves. Anyone else who attempts to enter — be they penniless pilgrims or Elector Counts — suffer the same treatment. The cause of peace recognises no authority higher than their own self-imposed mission, and while they themselves are peaceful, impatient troops could quickly turn the scene into a bloodbath. The Characters will have to exercise restraint and diplomacy to prevent a massacre here — a massacre which, perhaps, has been intended all along by certain cultists who have infiltrated the group.

In Boca Lupo

There are varying opinions as to what should be done. The Sigmarites would like the beast killed or driven off. A few of the Ulricans would like it left alone — they see it’s presence at the Shrine as either divine, or perhaps more cynically, as a good draw for pilgrims once word gets around.

Others would like to hunt it for themselves, and while long tradition says they should stalk the beast alone, most are not above paying for a little help — even more so once the disappearances start to mount. Of course, should a Character slay the beast then the honour and blessing of Ulric would fall upon them, along with the right to wear the pelt of a white wolf openly. An invitation to join a knightly order, especially the Order of the White Wolf, is certain to follow.

A White Wolf has recently been spotted near the Shrine, leaving almost everyone a touch nervous. Most see the wolf as more or less what it appears to be — a particularly large and likely dangerous wolf. Even the Ulricans are concerned, as while the White Wolf is sacred to Ulric and sometimes seen as protectors, they are more often aggressors, representatives of the harsher nature of the God of War, Winter and Wolves.

The slaying of such a beast is a religious experience in its own right, and a recent influx of hopeful young pilgrims has arrived hoping to win temporal fame and spiritual favour. In particular, an uncommon pilgrimage of Tilean worshippers of Ulric has arrived, desperete to slay the beast. Unfortunately for them, the creature has proven difficult to track and even more difficult to slay, and already a handful of hopeful hunters have vanished in the forests surrounding the shrine.
